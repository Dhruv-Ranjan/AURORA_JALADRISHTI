How the AI estimate is built

This is a multi-stage Earth-observation pipeline, not a live sensor reading or a single neural-network guess. The current 30-day results are experimental projections from real satellite scenes; each stage below is identified so the displayed risk can be interpreted with the right level of caution.

1. Real satellite observations

Scenes come from Sentinel-2 Level-2A via Microsoft Planetary Computer. The runner searches recent, low-cloud scenes and keeps each sample within its named region and MGRS tile. Published estimates use five or six usable dates; incomplete or unscorable samples are excluded rather than filled with synthetic values.

2. Spectral reconstruction

SpectralNetV10 is a multiscale residual encoder-decoder. It receives Sentinel-2 blue, green, and red imagery and estimates a six-channel spectrum: visible bands plus near-infrared and two shortwave-infrared bands. The hidden bands are model reconstructions, not direct NIR/SWIR inputs to the drought classifier.

3. Thirty-day projection

For each pixel and reconstructed band, a linear trend is fit across that location's available dates, then extrapolated 30 days past the newest scene. This creates a projected spectrum for the target date. Scene spacing is irregular, and this extrapolation can miss abrupt weather or land-use changes.

4. Physics-temporal drought model

The deployed PhysicsTemporalDroughtModel combines spatial convolution features, temporal convolutions, a bidirectional GRU with attention, and hand-built spectral features. Those features include NDVI, NDMI, NDWI, NBR, SAVI, and moisture ratios. It scores recent reconstructed scenes together with the projected target spectrum.

5. Risk index, not probability

The displayed 0-100 composite risk index blends the classifier output with a rules-based spectral stress score. It is useful for ranking these regions, but it is not a calibrated percent chance of drought. Cards show the model and physics components separately and identify sparse-land fallback scoring.

6. Confidence and validation limits

Uncalibrated confidence-head output is trained against a heuristic target: 65% comes from how far the physics-derived training label is from neutral, and 35% from how far the model's physics prior is from neutral. It is not calibrated against prediction correctness, so a displayed 30% does not mean a 30% chance of being right. In this batch its mean is 35%, median 31%, and 64 of 185 samples are below 30%; values were not increased. An exploratory candidate-model check across nine held-out regions found the raw head lower on correct outputs (33%) than incorrect outputs (48%); the test was reused for checkpoint selection and its labels are proxies, so this is diagnostic, not final calibration. Also, 151 of 185 samples used sparse-land fallback scoring, which has less vegetation-specific evidence. Training labels are physics-derived spectral labels, not independent field measurements. Development validation reported 93.1% accuracy and 0.953 AUC on a random, non-region-aware split; that does not establish performance on unseen regions or validate the 30-day extrapolation.
